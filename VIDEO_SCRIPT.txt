SoundBubble: video narration

Ever been in a conversation where two people talk at once, and later you can't remember who said what? SoundBubble picks one person's voice out of overlapping speech and then lets you search everything that person said. Each of us first records a short reference of our own voice, about ten seconds, using different words from the conversation that follows. That reference is all the model needs: this is target-speaker extraction, where the reference tells the model whose voice to keep. It doesn't sense direction or distance and doesn't control your headphones; it works on recorded audio. We then record a fresh conversation on this laptop's microphone with both of us talking over each other, pick whose voice to focus on, and click Extract. Under the hood is a BSRNN target-speaker-extraction model from the open-source REAL-TSE/WeSep project, running on the CPU with no GPU. We benchmarked four public checkpoints on our own voices and chose the best, which improved separation by about 16 to 19 dB on overlapping speech. We also built a two-speaker gate: because both of us are enrolled, stretches of the output that sound more like the other person are muted, which fixed the case where we take turns instead of overlapping. You can switch between the original recording and each extracted voice on the same playhead, and every number on screen is measured: processing time, real-time factor, and a speaker-match check that warns if the model grabbed the wrong voice. This is record-and-replay, not live listening, and on this CPU it takes about one and a half times the clip length per speaker. When we click Save, each separated voice is transcribed with Whisper, so every line already knows who said it, and each line becomes a timestamped segment in Elastic Cloud. Each segment stores the speaker, start and end times, the text, a 384-dimension embedding, and a pointer to the processed audio. The live captions and speaker lanes you see during playback are those Elasticsearch segments shown at the playhead, lit up by each extracted track's real audio level. Search runs entirely in Elasticsearch: one query combines BM25 keyword matching on the transcript with kNN vector search on the embeddings, filtered by speaker or conversation, and comes back in tens of milliseconds. So we can ask it to find where Yuvraj talked about Kubernetes networking, click the result, and it plays exactly that moment from his separated voice. The search and storage service runs on our Kubernetes cluster, connected to Radar. Our coding agent worked through Radar's MCP server: it discovered the cluster, dry-ran the manifests on the server before shipping, deployed, and read the pod logs through Radar to confirm the app was reaching Elastic Cloud. We then asked Radar's AI investigator to check the running app. It confirmed the service was healthy, with no restarts and passing health checks, and flagged a real security issue: the pod was mounting a Kubernetes API token it never uses. We turned that off, Radar watched the rollout, and the security audit dropped from three findings to two. We kept the other two on purpose, because the models need a writable filesystem and our data volume attaches to only one pod. That's SoundBubble: a short reference picks the voice, the model separates it, Elastic remembers it, and Radar keeps the service healthy. Today it's record-and-replay; live listening is next.
